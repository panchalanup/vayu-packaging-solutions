# Single, Double and Triple Wall Corrugated: How to Choose

Ask three suppliers for a "strong box" and you may get three different boards. The first thing to pin down is the **wall**: how many fluted layers the board has.

This guide explains single, double and triple wall board, what each is good for, and how to decide without paying for strength you do not need.

**Next step:** compare 3, 5 and 7-ply on your own box size in the [3D Box Designer](/box-designer), or [ask us for a quote](/quote).

## Wall and ply: the same idea, two names

A **wall** is one fluted (wavy) layer sandwiched between flat liners. The **ply** is the total count of paper layers. So:

| Wall | Ply | Construction |
|---|---|---|
| Single wall | **3-ply** | liner, flute, liner |
| Double wall | **5-ply** | liner, flute, liner, flute, liner |
| Triple wall | **7-ply** | liner, flute, liner, flute, liner, flute, liner |

Each added wall makes the board thicker and stiffer, and it adds paper, so it adds cost and weight too.

## Typical thickness

Thickness depends on the flutes used, so these are approximate.

| Board | Approximate thickness |
|---|---|
| 3-ply | 3 to 5 mm |
| 5-ply | 6 to 8 mm |
| 7-ply | 10 to 15 mm |

Thickness matters for strength, because a thicker board resists crushing better. It also takes a few millimetres off the **inside** of your box, which is why you should always specify [inside dimensions](/blogs/box-measurements-guide).

## Single wall (3-ply)

The everyday board. Light, economical, and printable.

**Good for:** e-commerce parcels, apparel, books, light retail goods, inner cartons and short stacks.

**Falls short when:** the box is heavy, stacked tall or stored for long in humid conditions, or the journey has many handling points.

## Double wall (5-ply)

Two flutes give a much stiffer wall. For the same box size, 5-ply carries far more stacking load than 3-ply.

**Good for:** appliances and electronics, heavier goods, godown stacking, and most export cartons.

**Worth knowing:** a double wall can be a better buy than a much heavier 3-ply, because stiffness comes from board thickness and flute structure as well as paper weight. Compare both in an estimate.

## Triple wall (7-ply)

The heavy-duty board: thick, rigid and slower to cut and fold.

**Good for:** machinery parts, very heavy or bulky products, bulk bins and tall pallet stacks.

**Worth knowing:** triple wall is sometimes used instead of wooden crates, but check weight, handling and the buyer's rules before you decide. Cartons for export may travel on wooden pallets, and those pallets (not the cartons) have their own treatment and marking rules, usually called ISPM 15. Your freight forwarder will tell you what the destination needs.

## How much does the wall change the strength?

A box's stacking strength depends on the board's edge crush strength (ECT), its thickness and the size of the box. The usual estimate is the McKee formula:

> box compression strength ≈ 5.87 × ECT × √(board thickness × box perimeter)

A thicker board raises both ECT and thickness, which is why going from 3-ply to 5-ply increases strength a lot, not a little. [Will my box stack?](/blogs/box-stacking-strength-guide) walks through a worked example, and the designer's strength guidance does the maths for your size. The figures it shows are **indicative**: real strength depends on the paper grades, humidity and handling.

## A simple way to choose

| If your box is... | Start with |
|---|---|
| Light, single, shipped by courier | 3-ply |
| Heavier, fragile, or stacked in a godown or pallet | 5-ply |
| Very heavy, bulky or on a demanding route | 7-ply |

Then adjust for four things:

- **Stack height and storage time.** The box at the bottom carries all the others.
- **Humidity.** Paper loses strength when damp. Monsoon storage justifies a stronger spec.
- **Journey.** More handling points and longer distances call for more margin.
- **Contents.** A rigid product (tins, bottles in partitions) shares the load; soft goods do not.

If you are close to the line, compare the next ply up in the designer's [price estimate](/blogs/box-price-estimate-guide). Sometimes better paper in the same ply is the cheaper fix, see the [GSM guide](/blogs/gsm-calculation-strength).

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a wall by the product weight alone.** Stacking and storage often decide it.
- **Going to triple wall "to be safe".** It adds cost, weight and slower packing.
- **Ignoring flutes.** A 5-ply box with B+C flutes behaves differently from one with two B flutes: see [flute types](/blogs/flute-types-guide).
- **Not testing.** Pack a real sample, load it and stack it before a large order.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 5-ply always double wall?
Yes. 5-ply means five paper layers: three liners and two flutes, which is a double wall board.

### Is 3-ply strong enough for 10 kg?
It is on the line. The designer treats about 8 kg as a comfortable maximum for 3-ply, so 10 kg usually calls for 5-ply unless the box is single, unstacked and the contents are rigid. Enter the weight in the strength guidance for an indicative answer, and test a sample.

### Does 7-ply cost twice as much as 3-ply?
Board cost rises with every layer, but the exact difference depends on paper grades and box size. Compare them in the designer's estimate, then confirm in a quote.

### What is "wall" vs "ply" in a quote?
They describe the same construction in two ways. Always write both, for example "5-ply (double wall), B+C flute".
