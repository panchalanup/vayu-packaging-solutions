# Fruit and Vegetable Boxes: Ventilated Cartons, Open Trays and Hand Holes Explained

A plain sealed carton is a poor home for fresh produce. Fruit and vegetables are alive: after harvest they give off warmth and moisture, and a closed box traps both. That is why produce boxes have **vent slots** and **hand holes**. They let air move through the pack and let a picker, packer or porter lift a full box without it slipping.

The catch is that every hole takes board away from the walls, and the walls are what hold the stack up. This guide explains that trade-off honestly and shows how to plan a ventilated carton or an open tray in the free **3D Box Designer**, where the openings appear on the model and the true cut lines appear on the dieline.

## The problem: air, weight and stacking pull in different directions

- **Airflow.** Fruit needs air around it in a truck, a mandi shed, a cold room or a shop.
- **Strength.** Produce is heavy and dense, and boxes are stacked several high, sometimes for days.
- **Handling.** Most produce boxes are lifted by hand many times between farm and shop.

More vents help airflow but weaken the walls. Bigger hand holes help handling but weaken the walls. Thicker board restores strength but costs more. The practical job is to find a sensible middle: **enough openings to do their work, no more**, on a board that still carries the load.

## What vents and hand holes do to strength

A corrugated box carries a stacking load mostly through its **walls and corners**, like four columns. Cutting a slot in a column removes material exactly where the load travels. General packaging literature reports that vent and hand holes can reduce a carton's compression (stacking) strength by roughly **10% to 40%**, depending on the size, shape, position and number of openings.

General rules of thumb, not specific to any one box:

- **Position matters.** Openings near the top or bottom edges or the vertical corners usually cost more strength than openings in the middle of a wall.
- **Shape matters.** Openings with rounded corners tend to be kinder than sharp-cornered rectangles, because stress gathers at sharp corners.
- **Several small beats one big.** Small openings spread out are usually better than one large one.
- **More air is not always better.** Produce-cooling reports suggest the benefit levels off beyond a modest share of wall area, often quoted at around 7-8%, so extra slots mostly cost strength.

<!-- VERIFY-LATER[BLOG-71]: the 7-8% vent-area rule of thumb is from general literature (designer constant VENT_AREA_USEFUL_PCT, DSG-72). Confirm with a packaging engineer or cold-chain adviser before using it as advice for a specific crop. -->

The designer builds this honesty in. With vents or hand holes switched on, the strength guide shows a plain warning and treats its stack figure as a **guide, not a promise**. It does not subtract an exact percentage, because the real loss depends on the layout and only a sample compression test gives a reliable answer.

**What to do about it:**

1. **Keep openings modest:** enough for airflow and a comfortable grip.
2. **Step up the board** if the stack is tall or the load heavy. See [wall construction](/blogs/corrugated-wall-differences) and [box stacking strength](/blogs/box-stacking-strength-guide).
3. **Test a sample**, loaded and stacked for as long as the real boxes will sit.
4. **Remember moisture.** Board loses strength when damp, and fresh produce is damp. A box that is strong in a dry warehouse may behave differently in a humid cold room. Say so when you ask for a quote.

## Step by step: designing a ventilated fruit carton

### 1. Open the fruit box preset

Go to the [fruit box designer](/box-designer/fruit-box). It opens a ventilated produce carton with vents and hand holes on, in natural kraft and 5-ply (B and C flutes). Or open **Templates** and choose the **Mango carton**, **Grape box** or **Citrus carton**.

### 2. Set the inside size from the fruit

In the **Edit Box** tab, enter the **inside length, width and height**. Lay out one layer of fruit the way you will pack it (loose, in a tray or in paper wrap) and measure the footprint. Add the number of layers, any pulp tray between layers and a little space at the top. The tool adds board thickness and shows the outside size. The fit checker in the same tab can suggest a size from one product's measurements; see [finding the right box size](/blogs/find-right-box-size).

| Template starting sizes (inside, cm) | Notes |
|---|---|
| Mango carton 36 x 26 x 14 | Described in the tool as a 5 kg-style carton |
| Grape box 30 x 20 x 12 | Described as a 2 kg-style box |
| Apple tray 40 x 30 x 12 | Open ventilated tray |
| Citrus carton 40 x 30 x 20 | Vented, with hand holes |
| Vegetable crate 50 x 35 x 20 | Strong open tray |

<!-- VERIFY-LATER[BLOG-72]: the "5 kg-style" and "2 kg-style" descriptions are the designer's own template blurbs and depend on fruit size and packing method. Confirm with production what net weights these sizes really hold before quoting them as pack sizes. -->

These are **starting points**, not standards. Fruit varies by variety, season and grade, so always measure your own.

### 3. Switch on vents and hand holes, then try them off

In **Edit Box**, find the **Cut-outs** section: **Hand holes** (a hand slot in each short wall), **Vent slots** (airflow and cooling) and, on some styles, a **Window**. Toggle them and watch the model; openings are drawn as dark cut-outs with shaded inner edges. A carton that looks right with hand holes and a few vents may look over-cut with everything on.

![The ventilated produce carton with Hand holes and Vent slots switched on in the Cut-outs section](/blog-images/designer/designer-produce-vents.png)
*Cut-outs section: hand holes and vent slots shown on the model.*

### 4. Choose the board to match the openings

Set the **ply** and **flute**. As a starting point: **3-ply** for light produce in short stacks (berries, a shallow tray of greens); **5-ply** for heavier fruit, taller stacks and truck or pallet loads (the preset); **7-ply** for very heavy loads or bulk bins. Natural kraft is the usual look for produce and prints well in one or two colours.

### 5. Put your farm or brand name where buyers see it

Open the **Customize** tab and add your name, village or brand as text and your logo as an image, on the wall that faces outward in a stack. The **Cutouts** panel offers ready-made fruit and vegetable graphics you can drop on any wall, resize, recolour in one tap and repeat as a pattern. Keep text and logos clear of vents, hand holes and folds; the print checks warn you.

### 6. Check strength, price and the dieline

Open **Price & order**. Enter the weight of one packed box and read the strength guide: a verdict about the board, how many boxes high to stack, and the warning about vents and hand holes. Cut-outs add a small die cost to the indicative estimate.

![The strength panel for a ventilated carton, with a warning that hand holes and vents reduce stacking strength](/blog-images/designer/designer-produce-strength-warning.png)
*The strength guide with vents and hand holes on: the warning is part of the answer.*

Then open **Actions** and download the **Dieline (SVG, 1:1)**. It shows the **true cut lines** for slots and holes in millimetres, so your die maker works from a precise drawing. See [dielines explained](/blogs/dielines-explained).

### 7. Share it and ask for a quote

Send the **share link** or the **spec sheet PDF**, then send the design to us. Mention the crop, net weight, how it is stored and moved, whether it goes into cold storage and the stack height you expect. Those details change the board advice more than anything else.

## Open trays: when a lid is not needed

An **open produce tray** has low walls, vents and no top flaps. It suits soft fruit in a single layer, apples and pears for display and loose vegetables. It uses less board and is quick to fill, but gives no protection from above, and the tray above rests on the walls of the tray below, so wall strength still matters. For long journeys, consider stacking trays inside a larger carton or wrapping the stack with film.

## Tips for produce boxes

- **Match opening size to the fruit.** Grapes and berries can push through big slots.
- **Print one side clearly:** the wall that faces the aisle or the truck door.
- **Keep one footprint across sizes** so boxes stack together and pallets stay stable.
- **Do not leave wet fruit on bare board** for long. Paper wrap, pulp trays or liners help; ask your supplier.
- **Test, then order.** Run a small sample, load it, stack it and watch it before a full season order.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do vents and hand holes make a box weaker?

Yes. General literature reports a reduction in stacking strength of roughly 10-40%, depending on size, shape, position and number. The designer warns you and treats its stack figure as a guide. Test a sample if the load is heavy or the stack tall.

### How many vent slots does a produce box need?

It depends on the crop, pack size and storage. Aim for modest openings that give airflow without removing much wall area. Your supplier or a cold-chain adviser can suggest a layout for your fruit.

### Open tray or closed carton?

A tray is quick to pack and uses less board, but protects and stacks less. A closed carton protects the fruit and stacks better, so it is the safer choice for long journeys.

### Is corrugated board safe for fresh produce?

It depends on whether the fruit touches the board. Tell us what you are packing and how, and we will discuss suitable options. The designer helps with size, style and print; it does not certify materials.
