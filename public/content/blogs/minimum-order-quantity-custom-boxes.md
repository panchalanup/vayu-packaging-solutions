# Minimum Order Quantity for Custom Boxes: Why and Options

Custom boxes have a minimum order because each new size and print needs one-time setup: printing plates, a cutting die for die-cut styles and machine changeover. That cost is the same for 100 or 5,000 boxes. Our current minimum is 500 boxes, to be confirmed against your spec. If you need fewer, there are honest options.

*Last checked: October 2026. The minimum and every figure below are indicative; your quote confirms them.*

[Compare 100, 500, 1,000 and 5,000 boxes in the designer](/box-designer?style=rsc&l=30&w=22&h=15&ply=3-ply&fl=B)

If you sell a few hundred units a month, a minimum order can feel like an obstacle. It helps to know what it is made of, because once you can see the cost, you can design around it instead of arguing with it. This guide explains where the minimum comes from, what it looks like in the designer's estimate, and what you can do if your first order is smaller than the minimum.

## Why a minimum order exists

Making one box is not much cheaper than making fifty. The expensive part is getting the job ready:

- **Printing plates.** Flexographic printing normally needs a plate for each ink colour. A one-colour logo needs one plate; a design with four colours needs four. Plates are made once per artwork.
- **A cutting die.** Die-cut styles such as mailers, pizza boxes and trays are cut with a die made to the exact shape. A slotted carton (RSC) does not need one. Hand holes, vents and windows on slotted styles can need a smaller one.
- **Machine setup.** The corrugator and the converting machines are adjusted for your board, size and print. Time spent adjusting is not time spent making boxes.
- **Trim and start-up waste.** Some board is always lost at the start of a run and at the trim, and a short run spreads that loss over very few boxes.
- **Handling.** Every separate job needs scheduling, stacking, bundling and loading, however small.

Add them up and you get a fixed cost per job. A minimum order exists so that this fixed cost is shared across enough boxes for the price per box to be fair for both sides.

## What setup does to the price per box

The designer's estimate spreads one-time setup over the quantity you choose, so the effect is visible. Read the table sideways, not down. Two things are happening at once:

1. **Setup is spread thinner.** At 100 boxes about a fifth of the per-box figure is setup; at 5,000 it is almost nothing.
2. **Volume lowers the other costs a little.** The estimate also applies a volume factor to board, print and conversion, so large runs cost less per box even apart from setup.

The biggest drop is from 100 to 500. Beyond about 1,000 the curve flattens, which is why a very large first order is rarely worth the storage and cash it ties up. ## Honest ways to order fewer boxes

Some of these may not apply to your product, and none of them is a promise. They are the right questions to ask any supplier, including us. <!-- VERIFY-LATER[BLOG-406]: confirm which of these Vayu actually offers: an existing size or stock box, plain boxes without print, orders delivered in parts, and any short-run print method. Remove any that are not offered. -->

### 1. Ask whether an existing size fits

If a size the supplier already makes fits your product, you skip the cost of a new size. It may not be exactly what you would design, but a box that is 1 cm bigger than ideal at a lower setup cost can be the better business decision.

### 2. Order plain boxes and brand them yourself

A plain kraft or white box has no printing plates at all: in the designer, a design with no print shows no plate setup. You can add your brand with a label, a sticker, a stamp or branded tape. It will not look like a fully printed box, but for a first batch, a test market or a low-value product it is often enough. The [packaging mistakes guide](/blogs/packaging-mistakes-small-business) lists other places small sellers overspend.

### 3. Print fewer colours

Each colour needs a plate. A one-colour logo on kraft is the cheapest way to look branded. In the same estimate, changing the 40 x 30 x 25 cm example from one colour to a four-colour image at 500 boxes raises the indicative per-box figure by roughly a third, because there are four plates and more ink area.

### 4. Choose a slotted style over a die-cut one

A mailer looks better than an RSC for many products, but it needs a die. If the box is mostly seen by couriers rather than customers, a slotted carton avoids that cost. Compare both on your size in the designer and read the difference in the estimate.

### 5. Use fewer sizes

Each size is its own setup. If you sell three products that could share two box sizes, you pay for two setups, not three, and each size reaches the minimum sooner. The [fit checker](/blogs/find-right-box-size) shows how much padding a slightly bigger box would add.

### 6. Ask about delivery in parts

If your problem is storage or cash, not the number itself, ask whether one order can be produced together and delivered in two or three lots. Not every supplier agrees, and pricing may differ, so ask before you assume.

### 7. Ask about short-run print

Some print methods suit small runs better than others. Which ones are available, at what minimum, varies by supplier and by your artwork. We do not list one here until it is confirmed, so ask.

## How to choose your first quantity

A quantity that is too low means reorders, repeated setup and a price per box that stays high. One that is too high ties up cash, takes storage space and risks artwork changes making stock obsolete. A simple way to pick:

1. **Estimate monthly use** from your last three months of orders.
2. **Multiply by the months you want covered**, usually two to four.
3. **Check storage:** boxes arrive flat, but they still need dry space.
4. **Compare two slabs** in the estimate and see how much the per-box figure falls. If it falls only slightly, take the smaller one.
5. **Keep your artwork stable** for that period. A new logo makes unused printed stock waste.

If you are not sure of your volume, choose the smaller slab that meets the minimum and reorder.

## Questions to ask about any minimum

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is the minimum per size, per print design, or per order? | Three sizes at 500 each is 1,500 boxes |
| Is it different for plain and printed boxes? | Plain boxes skip plates |
| Are plates, die and proof charged separately or in the unit price? | You need this to compare quotes |
| Are plates and die kept for a reorder? | A reorder may cost less than the first order |
| Can an order be delivered in parts? | Helps storage and cash |
| What changes at the next slab? | Tells you whether to stretch the quantity |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the minimum order for custom printed boxes?

Our current minimum order is 500 boxes, to be confirmed against your size, style and print. The designer's 100-box column is a planning view of short runs, not an offer. Ask us for your spec.

### Can I order 100 boxes?

The designer shows what 100 boxes would cost in an estimate, because the fixed setup makes the per-box figure much higher. Whether we can accept a run that small for your box depends on the style and print, so ask. A plain box or a stock-size box is the more likely route for small quantities.

### Why is the price per box higher on small orders?

Setup is a one-time cost that does not depend on quantity. On a small order it is spread over few boxes, so each box carries a larger share. On a large order the same cost nearly disappears.

### Is there a minimum for plain boxes with no print?

It can be lower, because there are no plates, but the board, size and cutting still take setup. Confirm it for your size when you ask for a quote.

### Does a bigger order always mean a better price?

Up to a point. The biggest saving is usually between the smallest and middle slabs; after that each step saves less. A very large order can also tie up cash and storage and leave you with stock if the artwork changes.
