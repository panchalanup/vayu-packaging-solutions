# For Box Manufacturers: Win More Orders by Showing Customers Their Box in 3D

If you make corrugated boxes, you already know the hardest part of a new order is often not the corrugator, the printer or the die. It is the conversation before any of that: getting a customer to agree on the box they actually want.

A buyer says "make it like the last one, but a bit taller". Another wants "something premium" and cannot say what that means. A third approves a size over the phone, then rejects the first batch because the logo sits too close to the flap. Every one of these costs you a sample, a reprint, or the order itself.

This guide shows how a free, browser-based **3D Box Designer** can act as your showroom: on a laptop across the table, on a tablet at a trade fair, or as a link you send after a call. No sign-up, nothing to install.

## The problem: customers buy what they can picture

Most packaging buyers are not packaging people. They run a kitchen-appliance brand, a spice business or an online clothing store. When you say "RSC, 5-ply, 300 × 200 × 150 inside, two-colour flexo on the long panels", many of them nod politely and hope for the best.

That gap shows up in familiar ways:

- **Slow approvals.** The quote sits in someone's inbox because nobody on their side can visualise it well enough to sign off.
- **Late changes.** The customer only "sees" the box when the sample arrives, and that is when the change requests start.
- **Price shopping.** If your offer is a line of text, it is easy to compare against a cheaper line of text from someone else.
- **Disputes after delivery.** "This is not what we agreed" is hard to answer when what you agreed was a phone call.

A 3D preview does not replace your experience. It gives the customer something to point at, and it gives you a record of what they pointed at.

## When a 3D presentation helps most

You do not need the designer for every repeat order of a plain brown carton. It earns its place when:

1. **The customer is new** and has never bought custom boxes before.
2. **Branding is involved**: a logo, a printed inside, a brand colour or a gift-style finish.
3. **The style is unfamiliar** to the buyer: a die-cut mailer, a telescope lid and base, a pizza-style box or a tray.
4. **Several people must approve**: the founder, the marketing person and the purchase manager all have opinions.
5. **You are pitching against other suppliers** and want your offer to look more considered.

## Step by step: a client meeting with the designer

Here is a simple flow you can run in ten to fifteen minutes with a customer.

### 1. Start from the closest style

Open the designer and pick the style that matches the brief: regular slotted carton (RSC), half slotted (HSC), full overlap (FOL), mailer, telescope (lid and base), sleeve, pizza or tray. The style picker shows each one as a card with a short "best for" note, which is a useful way to explain the options to a buyer who does not know the names.

![The style picker showing box styles as cards with a short best-for note](/blog-images/designer/designer-style-picker.png)
*The style picker: each card names the style and what it suits, so the customer can choose by looking rather than by FEFCO code.*

### 2. Enter the size in the customer's unit

Type the inside length, width and height. If the buyer thinks in inches and your shop floor works in millimetres, switch units in the designer; the box does not change, only the numbers. Ask the customer to watch the model while you adjust: it is often the moment they realise the height they asked for is too generous.

### 3. Set the board and finish

Choose 3, 5 or 7-ply and a board colour. Kraft, white and coloured options, matte or gloss, and a different inside colour (for example a white inside on a kraft outside) are all visible on the model. The strength guidance gives an indicative verdict on how much load the box can take and how high it can stack, which is a useful talking point, though you will still confirm the final board spec from your own experience and testing.

### 4. Put their logo on it

Drop their logo onto the face they choose, move it, resize it, add a line of text. This is usually the moment the meeting changes tone: the box stops being a quotation and starts being *theirs*.

### 5. Switch to presentation mode

Presentation mode hides the panels and shows the box full screen on a slow turntable, with a large spec overlay. Use the arrow keys to step through the fold animation or between variants you prepared ("kraft with one colour" versus "white with full-colour print"). On a tablet propped on the table, this looks far more like a product launch than a quotation.

![Presentation mode showing a branded box on a turntable with a large spec overlay](/blog-images/designer/designer-presentation-mode.png)
*Presentation mode: no panels, a slow turntable and the key specs in large type, made for showing a box across a table.*

### 6. Send a proof for approval

Export the **spec sheet / proof PDF**. It is one page with three views of the box, the dimensions in the chosen unit, board, finish and quantity, a dieline thumbnail, the date and a customer approval block for a signature. Email it after the meeting or print it on the spot.

![A one-page proof PDF with three views, dimensions, board and a signature block](/blog-images/designer/designer-spec-sheet-pdf.png)
*The proof PDF: three views, dimensions, board and finish, and a block for the customer to sign and date.*

### 7. Share the link

Every design can be saved and shared as a link. The customer can open it later on their own phone, rotate the box and forward it to colleagues. When they come back with a change, open their link, make the change and send a new proof.

## Tips from the sales table

- **Prepare two or three variants before the meeting.** Save them as separate designs (for example plain kraft, one-colour print, full-colour white). Comparing side by side is easier than imagining.
- **Let the customer drive.** Hand them the tablet and let them rotate the box. People trust what they have touched.
- **Use the fold animation to explain the style.** A mailer or a telescope box makes sense instantly when the customer watches it fold.
- **Show the inside.** The cut-away view hides the front wall so you can talk about inserts, padding and the printed inside.
- **Put a size reference in the scene.** The A4 sheet or 1-litre bottle silhouette helps buyers judge real size, which a screen otherwise hides.
- **Date every proof.** When the design changes, send a new proof rather than editing the old one, so there is a clear history.
- **Keep your own costing in charge.** Treat the tool's estimate as a talking point and quote from your own sheet.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the 3D Box Designer really free for box manufacturers?

Yes. It runs in the browser, needs no sign-up and has no paid tier. You can use it to design and present boxes to your customers.

### Do my customers need to install anything to open a shared design?

No. A shared link opens in any modern browser on a laptop, tablet or phone.

### Can I show the designer offline at a trade fair?

The designer needs an internet connection to load. If the venue Wi-Fi is unreliable, use a mobile hotspot, or export product shots and the turntable video beforehand as a backup.

### Is the price shown in the tool what I should quote?

No. Prices in the designer are indicative and labelled so. Use them as a guide in conversation, then quote from your own costing.

### Can I use the dieline directly for my die?

Treat it as a starting drawing at 1:1 scale with cut and crease lines. Your die maker will adjust allowances for your board and machines.

### Where are my saved designs stored?

In "My designs" in your own browser. Nothing is uploaded unless you share a link or send an enquiry. Clearing browser data removes them, so export a proof PDF of anything important.

### Can my customer sign the proof digitally?

The proof PDF has an approval block for a name, signature and date. They can print and sign it, or sign it with any PDF tool they already use.
