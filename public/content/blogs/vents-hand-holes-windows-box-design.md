# Vents, Hand Holes and Windows: Cost and Strength Impact

Cut-outs are easy to love. A vented mango carton looks right. A hand hole makes a heavy box friendlier. A window on a cake box sells the cake before the lid opens. But every hole you cut in a corrugated box takes board away from the walls that carry the load, and it adds a step in production.

So the useful question is not "can I add one?" but **"does this opening earn its place?"** This guide explains the three kinds of opening, when each is worth having, what they cost in general terms, how they affect strength (honestly, with the limits of what we can say), and how to try them in the free 3D Box Designer before you commit.

## The three openings in plain words

| Opening | What it is | Typical job |
|---|---|---|
| **Vent slots** | Narrow openings cut in the walls | Let air through so fresh produce and flowers cool and breathe |
| **Hand holes** | A grip slot cut in the short walls | Make a heavy or bulky carton easy to lift and carry |
| **Window** | A cut-out in a lid or wall, usually covered with clear film | Let the buyer see the product, such as a cake, toy or earbuds |

They look similar on the dieline (a shape cut out of the blank), but they behave differently. Vents and hand holes are cut in walls that carry stacking load. A window in a lid usually sits in a panel that carries much less, though a large one still weakens it.

---

## When each one is worth adding

### Vents: when the product needs air
Fresh fruit and vegetables give off heat and moisture. In a sealed box, that builds up, and ripening and rot speed up. Vent slots let air move and let cool air reach the fruit. **Add vents when:** you pack fresh produce, flowers or anything that must breathe, especially for travel in warm weather or for cold storage. **Skip them when:** the product is dry and sealed, or the box is a stacked shipping carton for goods that do not need air.

### Hand holes: when people carry the box
Hand holes help when a box is lifted by hand often: hampers, sweets, appliances, hardware, plants, moving boxes, produce cartons in a market. **Add them when:** the box weighs enough to be awkward to grip, or it is carried a distance. **Skip them when:** the box will go onto a pallet in tall stacks, or the product is light and the box has flaps that make a natural grip.

### Windows: when sight sells
A window works when the product itself is the best advertisement: cakes, cookies, toys, kidswear, small electronics. **Add one when:** looking is part of buying. **Skip it when:** the product is fragile to light, the box is for courier travel (film can tear or scuff), or you want the lowest cost.

## How openings affect strength (the honest version)

A corrugated box carries stacking load mostly through its **walls and corners**. Take board out of those walls and the load has less board to travel through.

- **The range in the literature.** Packaging research on produce cartons reports that vent and hand holes can reduce a carton's compression strength, commonly quoted at roughly **10 to 40 percent**, depending on the size, number and position of the holes. We cannot give a single number for your box, because it depends on the board, the box proportions, how the holes are shaped and where they sit.
- **More holes is not more cooling.** Reported cooling gains tend to level off once vent area reaches about 7 to 8 percent of the wall area, so cutting far beyond that costs strength without helping the fruit.
- **Position matters.** As a general design rule, openings close to corners and creases do more harm than ones in the middle of a panel, and rounded ends are kinder to the board than sharp corners. Your supplier can advise on your layout.
- **Windows** in a lid cost less, but a large window can make a lid flimsy, and film adds a part that can be damaged.

The designer's strength guidance reflects this in plain words: when a design has hand holes or vents, it warns that stacking strength drops, often by an amount in that general range, and asks you to treat the stack figure as a guide and test a sample before a full order. If vents cover a large share of the walls, it notes that fewer slots keep more strength.

### How to protect strength when you need holes

- **Step up the board.** A stronger ply can win back part of what the openings take away. See [single, double and triple wall](/blogs/corrugated-wall-differences).
- **Use fewer, well-placed openings** instead of many.
- **Stack lower.** If the box is vented, a stack that would be fine for a plain carton may not be. Read [will my box stack?](/blogs/box-stacking-strength-guide).
- **Test a sample** under the real load, in the real humidity. Produce boxes sit in damp, cold rooms, and moisture weakens board further.
- **Talk to us about the layout.** The position of the cut can be changed on the die.

## What openings cost, in general terms

Each opening adds something to production, and the details vary by supplier and quantity:

- **A cutting die.** Slots, hand holes and windows need a die. Die-cut styles such as mailers, cake boxes and trays already carry one; for a slotted carton, adding holes can mean an added die cost, usually once per size.
- **Window film and gluing.** A film window adds a material and an assembly step per box. This is generally a small per-box cost, but over thousands of boxes it adds up.
- **Slower production.** Cutting and gluing add machine and labour time, which matters more at low volumes.
- **Recycling.** A plastic film window can complicate recycling where the film must be separated from the paper. Ask your supplier about alternatives if this matters to you.

The designer's estimate includes a small allowance for these, labelled as indicative. The real number depends on your supplier and run size, so compare the estimate with and without the opening and ask for a quote.

## Common mistakes

1. **Vents in every wall.** Cooling needs air to pass through, not holes everywhere. Vents on two opposite walls usually give that path.
2. **Hand holes too close to an edge.** A slot hard against the base or the top edge is awkward to grip and weakens the wall.
3. **A huge window on a courier box.** Film tears and the lid sags. Keep the window to the area you need.
4. **Forgetting the print.** Artwork behind a window or across a vent is wasted. Check the 3D model and dieline together.
5. **Never testing.** Always load-test a sample of a vented or hand-holed carton before a full order.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do vents really make a box much weaker?
They reduce compression strength, and published figures for vent and hand holes commonly run from about 10 to 40 percent depending on size and position. That is a literature range, not a result for your box. Test a sample.

### How big should vent slots be?
Large enough to move air, but small enough to keep the walls strong. Reported cooling benefits level off at roughly 7 to 8 percent of the wall area, so more than that is usually wasted.

### Can I have a window and vents on one box?
The designer offers what each style supports: windows on mailers, cake boxes and cartons, vents on produce styles. For other combinations, ask us.

### Does a window need plastic film?
Usually, to keep out dust and handling. Some windows are left as an open cut-out. Ask your supplier about options that suit your product and your recycling goals.
