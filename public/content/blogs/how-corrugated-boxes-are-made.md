# How Corrugated Boxes Are Made: Paper to Finished Carton

A corrugated box starts as reels of kraft paper. A machine called a corrugator presses one paper into waves (the flute) and glues it between flat liners to make board. The board is cut into sheets, printed, creased and slotted or die-cut, then folded and joined. Plants differ in detail; this guide describes the general process.

## The process on one page

| Stage | What happens | What you can control as a buyer |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Paper | Large reels of liner paper (flat) and fluting medium (to be rippled) are loaded | Paper grade, GSM and burst factor in your spec ([reading a box spec](/blogs/reading-box-spec-ply-bf-gsm)) |
| 2. Corrugating | Fluting paper is heated, pressed into flutes and glued to a liner; more layers are added for 5 and 7-ply | Ply and flute type |
| 3. Cutting the board | The board is trimmed to width, scored and cut into sheets | Sheet size follows your dieline |
| 4. Printing | Ink is applied to the sheet or to a paper that is later laminated | Number of colours and print route ([printing guide](/blogs/flexo-offset-digital-printing-corrugated-boxes)) |
| 5. Creasing and cutting | Slots and creases (slotted cartons) or a steel-rule die (mailers, trays) | Style and size |
| 6. Joining | The glue flap is glued, stitched or taped; flat boxes are bundled | Joint type, packing and pallet |
| 7. Checks | Size, bonding, moisture and sometimes strength tests | What tests you ask for and see |

## Stage 1: paper

Corrugated board is made almost entirely from paper. The flat outer and inner layers are **liners**; the rippled layer is the **fluting medium**. Papers differ in fibre, colour, weight (GSM, grams per square metre) and strength. A brown kraft liner is the usual outer face; a white-top liner gives a better surface for print. The [kraft paper grades guide](/blogs/kraft-paper-grades) explains the grade names buyers meet on quotes.

Paper arrives as heavy reels, and the reels are the main raw material of a box plant. That is why GSM and board spec drive price so much: paper is usually a large part of the cost of a corrugated box.

---

## Stage 2: the corrugator

The corrugator is a long machine, often described as a production line the length of a building. In plain steps:

1. **Single facer.** Fluting paper is heated and pressed between two corrugating rolls with ridged surfaces, which shapes it into flutes. A starch-based glue is applied to the flute tips and a liner is pressed on. The result is **single-face board**: one liner and one rippled layer.
2. **Double backer.** For ordinary 3-ply board, glue goes on the other side of the flutes and a second liner is added. For 5-ply, a second single-face web is made and the two are joined with a middle liner; 7-ply adds a third.
3. **Heating and cooling section.** The glued board travels between heated plates so the glue sets, then is held flat as it cools.
4. **Slitter-scorer.** Knives trim the board to width and make score lines along its length.
5. **Cut-off.** A knife cuts the moving board to sheet length, and the sheets are stacked.

| Board | Layers | Common name |
|---|---|---|
| 3-ply | liner, flute, liner | Single wall |
| 5-ply | liner, flute, liner, flute, liner | Double wall |
| 7-ply | three flutes between four liners | Triple wall |

See [flute types](/blogs/flute-types-guide) for the flute profiles and [wall differences](/blogs/corrugated-wall-differences) for how the plies compare.

### Why board weighs more than the papers added up

The fluting paper is longer than the flat board it sits in, because it is rippled. The ratio is called the **take-up factor**, commonly quoted around 1.3 to 1.5. As an example only: two 150 GSM liners and a 120 GSM fluting medium at a take-up of 1.4 give 150 + 150 + (120 x 1.4) = 468 GSM of board. See [GSM and strength](/blogs/gsm-calculation-strength) for the full worked example.

### Flute direction

Corrugation runs in one direction along the board. In a finished carton the flutes should run **vertically** up the walls, because the flutes act like tiny columns when boxes are stacked. Box makers set this when they lay out the dieline on the sheet. It is one reason the same box can be stronger or weaker depending on how it is cut, and why you should state the box height clearly.

## Stage 3: printing

Printing happens in one of three broad ways: ink printed directly onto the board by flexographic plates, a printed paper laminated onto the board for high-quality retail boxes, or digital printing for short runs. Which is right depends on quantity and artwork detail, covered in [flexo, offset or digital printing on corrugated](/blogs/flexo-offset-digital-printing-corrugated-boxes). Many ordinary shipping cartons are printed in one or two colours as part of the same line that slots them.

---

## Stage 4: creasing, slotting and die-cutting

How the box takes its shape depends on the style:

- **Slotted cartons** (RSC, HSC, FOL) are made on a machine that prints, creases and cuts the slots between flaps in one pass, which is why they are the economical choice for standard sizes.
- **Die-cut styles** (mailers, trays, pizza boxes, clamshells, slide boxes) are cut and creased in one press with a **cutting die**: steel rules set in a board, shaped to the dieline. A new shape needs a new die, which is part of setup cost ([minimum order quantity](/blogs/minimum-order-quantity-custom-boxes)).

The drawing that guides either route is the dieline, explained in [box dielines explained](/blogs/dielines-explained). The designer can download a dieline for any of its [20 box styles](/blogs/free-dieline-template-library-box-styles).

---

## Stage 5: joining and packing

A narrow **glue flap** joins the last panel to the first so the box becomes a flat tube that folds open at the customer's end. Joints are made three common ways: glue, metal **stitching** (staples), or tape. Glued joints are neat and usual for retail and food boxes; stitched joints are common on heavier cartons. Boxes are then counted, bundled flat, strapped and palletised for dispatch.

---

## Stage 6: checks

Plants check the things that go wrong most often: box size against the order, glue bonding between layers, moisture in the board, print position and, depending on the plant, strength measures such as burst, edge crush and box compression. Which checks a plant runs and what it can show you differs, so ask. The [burst and ECT guide](/blogs/burst-strength-ect-guide) says what to put in a spec and what the tests mean.

## What this means when you order

- **Spec in the words of the process.** "5-ply, B + C flute, kraft liners, 150 GSM, burst factor as quoted" tells the plant exactly what to run on the corrugator.
- **Setup is real.** Plates and dies take time to make, and the corrugator has to be set up for your board. That is why short runs cost more per box.
- **Dieline first.** Tell the plant your inside size and styles; they size the blank from it.
- **Ask what is made in-house.** Some makers run their own corrugator; others buy board and convert it. Both can be good, and a good maker will tell you which they are ([questions to ask a manufacturer](/blogs/choose-corrugated-box-manufacturer-ahmedabad)).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the wavy layer inside a corrugated box called?

It is the fluting, or fluting medium. The waves (flutes) give the board its stiffness and cushioning, and flute height varies by type, such as B, C and E flute.

### Why are most corrugated boxes brown?

Because the usual liners are kraft paper, which is naturally brown. White-top liners and printed laminates are used where a lighter surface or better print is needed.

### How long does it take to make a box order?

It depends on the plant, the quantity, whether new plates or a die are needed, and the queue. Ask your supplier for a lead time on your exact spec, and add time for sampling and artwork approval.

### Is corrugated board recyclable?

It is paper, and paper recycling streams commonly accept it, though accepted materials differ by place. Plastic tape, heavy coatings and some laminates can complicate recycling, so ask your maker if that matters to you.

### What is the difference between a die-cut box and a slotted carton?

A slotted carton is made on a machine that creases and slots the sheet, so it needs no shaped die. A die-cut box is cut by a steel-rule die in the shape of its dieline, which allows complex shapes such as mailers and trays.
